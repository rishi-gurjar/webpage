*What would automating agriculture actually look like?* I think we're going to get there so we have to think about this stuff deeply. 

Commodity crops provide most of people's energy and I consider them largely automated. They are grown at vast scales. Specialty crops are much smaller but are also much more archaic. I'd blame that partly on the fact that commodity crops are just a bigger market but for a 2x bigger market, labor cost is 10x bigger. I think the largest reason is that harvest is just much harder given the morphology and the type of agricultural object produced.

The price of specialty crops is high, even though it has decreased since the Green Revolution (when inflation-adjusted), forcing most poor people to stick to processed foods or carbohydrates. Rising input prices, limited federal support (pretty much all ag subsidies go to commodity crops), and a lack of insurance for 40% of specialty crops (Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance) cause this. That's why we're focusing on specialty crops.

Specialty crops present an interesting challenge. When designing robots for the home or any human-designed spaces, the total span of spaces is limited because every air fryer, every couch, every fridge that a robot has to interface with was designed by a human. The lighting is controlled and many things are somewhat standardized like door sizes, handle placement, etc. Plants and any biological system present a span that dwarves any human-designed system because of genetic and environmental variance. Even though crops are plants that have been genetically engineered *for standardization*, the same cultivar could look quite different plant-to-plant. This is made much more complicated by the fact that some crops are harvested destructively, like most wine grapes, while others need to be carefully handled, like strawberries. Tree nuts have hard shells that protect the kernel that we eat while others have very soft shells that are damaged by a high heat day. Some are planted in greenhouses while others in outdoor beds. And environmental variables like the angle of incidence of the sun, wind, rain, can make the same plant look completely different based on the time of day.

Luckily, plants are machines that follow a rule to generate themselves, so patterns exist. Each class of plant has a different morphology but they're unified (since they're all part of the same family) by key elements like leaves, stems, flowers, etc. ML is all about spotting patterns.

There are two ways to tackle this: engineering a robot to interact with plants or engineering plants to make them easier to deal with. Plants themselves, the way they are grown, and the infrastructure around crops are designed to make them easy for humans to harvest. Apples grown on flat trellises massively reduce the complexity of a robot embodiment needed to interact with it, since it doesn't need to deal with heavy occlusion or have to dig deep into dense canopies. In Washington and Ontario right now, virtually 100% of new commercial orchard installations are planted using high-density trellis systems. But redesigning plants takes time, so I'll put that off for now. 

It's better to focus on engineering robots to deal with plants, since technology has suddenly made that possible. **The optimization we're trying to make is designing a single embodiment that can complete the most high-economic-value tasks for the most productive specialty crops.** 

There are many dimensions of difference and some make it harder to use a single embodiment for everything. For example, almonds have shakers that shake nuts off the tree, sweepers to align nuts in the row, harvesters to picks them up, and trucks that move nuts to the huller/sheller. Bigger embodiments = bigger liability. Strawberries or table grapes require something a lot more delicate but complexity is a lot higher. I'm certain that in the near future (within 100 years), farms will have several embodiments working together to achieve a goal, probably with a hive mind type setup. As robots get smarter, its quality can go down and performance won't change. A really dumb robot needs super expensive and precise actuators, encoders, etc., but a smart one can realize its limitations and adapt. It would be amazing to have a robot that costs $100 that anyone can build across the world that would just start harvesting their crops. That would be fucking insane but we can definitely do that. Just give us a few years.

*What's the optimal way to train a robot to harvest?* (greatest economic value:effort ratio task) There are two main ways to train a robot to do something you want. Either you show it examples of you doing the task, through teleop or something like [UMI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10329) and have it imitate (with a wide enough training dataset distribution with various edge cases, it would become very good at a given task) or through reinforcement learning, where you reward it for a certain behavior until it learns to do the behavior you like.

We (I) essentially spent two months unsuccessfully trying to teach a robot to pick up a cube in a controlled indoor environment. At least we learned a bunch of random shit about robot training. That might be cope. But after making a breakthrough, we've had more rapid progress.

Initially we're starting with training policies to hit strawberryesque balls off a white rectangle, then picking them up. The Forager Mk 1 can move forward and backward and command two SO101 arms.
